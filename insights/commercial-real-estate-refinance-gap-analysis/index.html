Commercial Real Estate Refinance Gaps: Find the Real Constraint

A higher appraisal does not necessarily pay off a maturing loan. Before seeking more capital, find out whether cash flow, collateral value, or the lender's structure is limiting the proceeds.

A commercial real estate refinance gap is easy to describe: the new loan will not cover everything that must be paid at closing. The difficult part is determining why. A property may still have substantial market value and an operating history, but the proposed lender may size the replacement debt to the property's current income rather than to the original purchase price or the owner's equity.

This matters for borrowers facing maturities, acquisitions dependent on a near-term refinance, and owners considering whether to inject equity or negotiate an extension. The Mortgage Bankers Association estimated in February 2026 that $875 billion of commercial and multifamily mortgage balances were scheduled to mature during 2026, measured using unpaid balances at the end of 2025. That was a scheduled maturity total, not a count of loans remaining unrefinanced today. Treating the figure otherwise would exaggerate the current problem.

At a Glance

Diagnose before filling the gap

  • Calculate the full payoff requirement, including transaction costs and required reserves.
  • Size the proposed senior loan independently under DSCR, LTV and debt-yield constraints.
  • Identify which constraint binds; the lowest proceeds usually drive the preliminary estimate.
  • Test what changes the constraint, not merely what improves the spreadsheet.
  • Consider extensions, sponsor cash, an asset sale or permitted subordinate capital only after the primary math is clear.

What creates a refinance gap?

Start with sources and uses, not the value of the property. The sources are the money available to close: new senior debt, any additional capital and sponsor cash. The uses are the payoff of existing debt, financing costs, taxes and legal fees, required lender escrows, and any outstanding property needs that must be funded at closing.

A simplified formula is funding gap = total refinance uses − available senior loan proceeds, with a floor of zero. An accurate sources-and-uses schedule may include sale or transaction costs, reserves for tenant improvements or capital expenditures, and prepayment charges where applicable. It should separate current obligations from costs that are optional or can be staged.

Three tests that can limit the replacement loan

Loan-to-value (LTV) sets a ceiling based on the lender's accepted collateral value. At a maximum 65% LTV on an $8 million property, the illustrative cap is $5.2 million. A stronger appraisal could raise that ceiling, but it does nothing directly to improve operating cash flow.

Debt yield divides lender-underwritten net operating income (NOI) by the requested loan balance. At $520,000 NOI and a minimum 9% debt yield, the loan cap would be about $5.78 million. Debt yield is independent of the interest rate and amortization assumptions in the DSCR calculation.

Debt service coverage ratio (DSCR) compares annual underwritten NOI with annual required debt service. At $520,000 NOI and a minimum 1.25x DSCR, annual debt service cannot exceed $416,000. With a hypothetical 7.50% annual interest rate and 25-year monthly amortization, that supports approximately $4.69 million of senior debt. Other lender underwriting adjustments, such as vacancy, management fees, replacement reserves or stressed rates, could change that amount.

Illustrative Analysis · Not a Loan Quote

One property. Three different debt limits.

Assumptions: $8.00 million accepted value; $520,000 annual lender-underwritten NOI; 65% maximum LTV; 9% minimum debt yield; 1.25x minimum DSCR; 7.50% interest; 25-year amortization.

  • LTV debt cap: $5.20 million
  • Debt-yield debt cap: approximately $5.78 million
  • DSCR debt cap: approximately $4.69 million
  • Binding constraint: DSCR, before any additional underwriting limitations

If the existing loan payoff is $5.60 million and estimated refinance costs and required reserves total $180,000, the uses are $5.78 million. Against modeled senior proceeds of $4.69 million, the illustrative shortfall is approximately $1.09 million. The property still has $2.4 million in apparent equity based on value less existing debt; that equity is not equivalent to cash available at closing.

Which changes would actually close the gap?

Suppose the owner argues that interest rates are likely to decline. A lower rate could help because it reduces the annual payment on a fully amortizing loan and therefore increases the DSCR-based debt cap. But the LTV cap remains $5.2 million unless accepted value or permitted leverage changes. Even if DSCR were no longer the binding constraint, the modeled $5.78 million of closing uses still exceed that LTV limit.

Similarly, higher NOI improves debt-yield and DSCR calculations but not LTV by itself. It may support a higher appraisal eventually, depending on market evidence and valuation assumptions, but underwriting cannot assume both higher income and a more generous valuation without a defensible basis. Test each change separately first; then evaluate combinations with lender-specific policies.

The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency has emphasized the importance of evaluating commercial loan refinance risk at origination, during ongoing monitoring, and as maturity approaches. That is a useful discipline for property owners too: refinancing should be underwritten as an exit, not taken for granted as an event.

What a lender will want to reconcile

The difference between sponsor NOI and lender-underwritten NOI is often more important than a small change in rate. Ask which leases are signed but not paying rent, whether concessions remain, what vacancy assumption is applied, and whether management fees, taxes, insurance and recurring reserves have been normalized. A rent roll and trailing-12-month operating statement should reconcile rather than tell two different stories.

Also determine whether the proposed facility is fixed or floating, interest-only or amortizing, and whether it includes a balloon maturity. An interest-only payment may improve near-term coverage but does not erase principal at maturity. A lender's debt-service test may also stress the rate or require amortizing coverage even when some interest-only period is offered.

Practical options once the cause is known

Negotiate an extension early

An existing lender may consider an extension if the collateral is performing, the borrower can meet revised covenants and additional liquidity is available. An extension is not free capital; it can involve fees, principal paydowns, reserve increases or other concessions. Start that conversation before maturity pressure weakens the borrower's options.

Use sponsor equity only with a complete budget

Contributing additional cash may be the cleanest solution when the sponsor has adequate liquidity and the investment still merits further capital. The decision should account for upcoming leasing, repairs, taxes and operations. Filling the closing gap with the last available dollar can create a second problem immediately afterward.

Examine subordinate capital for total cost and permissions

Mezzanine debt, preferred equity and certain seller or partner structures may help fund a permitted shortfall. They can also introduce expensive returns, cash-flow pressure, intercreditor restrictions, control rights and securities-law considerations. Senior lenders may prohibit some structures altogether. Do not describe a hypothetical junior capital allocation as committed funding.

Consider a sale or recapitalization before the deadline

Where the property cannot support the proposed debt and additional equity is uneconomic, selling, bringing in an equity partner or changing the investment plan may be more defensible than extending the capital stack indefinitely. Timing matters: a voluntary strategic sale differs from a forced disposition after default.

Build one decision memo before approaching capital providers

A useful refinance package has a one-page summary of the transaction, a current rent roll, twelve months of operating results, a payoff statement, market-backed value evidence, lender sizing assumptions, sources and uses, capital-expenditure obligations, extension terms and sponsor liquidity. Include a downside case. Every proposed capital source should be labeled as available, proposed, or merely hypothetical.

If the property requires supplemental money, show precisely why the gap exists, what milestone would make the refinance viable later, how that milestone will be funded, and the consequence if it is delayed. A number without a credible exit path is not a capital strategy.

Frequently Asked Questions

Refinancing gaps, explained

Can a property have equity and still fail a refinance?

Yes. Equity based on market value does not ensure sufficient new loan proceeds. A lender may be constrained by NOI-based DSCR, debt yield, collateral value, loan policy or borrower requirements.

Does lowering the interest rate always close a refinance gap?

No. A lower rate can increase DSCR-based proceeds, but cannot independently remove an LTV or debt-yield ceiling, cure unpaid fees or provide committed junior capital.

Is the calculated gap the same as an approved equity requirement?

No. It is a scenario based on the selected assumptions. Actual requirements depend on the lender, verified financials, transaction costs, reserves, and permitted capital structure.
