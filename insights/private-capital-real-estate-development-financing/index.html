Private Capital for Real Estate Development: What a Financeable Project Needs

Construction financing is not one check against a finished building. It is a staged commitment to land, approvals, a budget, a team, a schedule, and an exit that must all hold together.

Stage 02Approvals
Stage 03Construction
Stage 04Takeout

A ground-up development can be attractive on a completed-value spreadsheet and still be unfinanceable today. The problem may be an unapproved site plan, missing utility capacity, an incomplete guaranteed maximum price, insufficient sponsor liquidity, or a construction schedule that runs longer than the proposed debt maturity. Before calling potential lenders and investors, the sponsor must make those risks visible and show who bears them.

For Leva Ventures, the starting point is not a list of capital sources. It is a disciplined review of the project itself: what has been approved, what is still conditional, what it will cost to finish, which milestones unlock financing, and what happens if the timeline slips.

Development Capital Readiness

Five workstreams have to agree

  • Land and approvals: ownership, zoning, entitlements, utilities, environmental matters and permits.
  • Development budget: hard costs, soft costs, financing expenses, interest carry and contingencies.
  • Execution: design, contractor, procurement, insurance, schedule and draw administration.
  • Capitalization: documented sponsor equity, staged lender advances and permissible partner contributions.
  • Exit: a supported permanent loan, investor recapitalization, phased sale or other lawful takeout.

Different phases attract different capital

Before entitlements, the principal risk may be whether the proposed project is legally and physically possible. Land acquisition and predevelopment capital can require equity or financing that is more expensive and less certain than a construction loan for a fully approved project.

After approvals, financing discussions shift toward execution: the land basis, the value of completed improvements, contractor performance, costs, sponsor experience and the evidence that an eventual buyer or permanent lender will be available. Construction lenders frequently control advances through inspections, lien waivers and draw conditions. A signed financing commitment is therefore not equivalent to cash sitting freely in the developer's operating account.

The OCC Commercial Real Estate Lending handbook discusses acquisition, development and construction risk, borrower capacity, feasibility, collateral and the importance of controls over construction progress. The FDIC's construction and land-development examination guidance likewise addresses draw controls and the relationship between estimated project completion and takeout timing. These are bank supervisory considerations, not a single universal lending formula for private capital.

Start with what is actually entitled

A parcel advertised as “shovel-ready” may still require site improvements, utility commitments, building permits, traffic studies, environmental remediation or agency approvals. Identify each approval by issuing authority, status, expiration date and condition. Separate what can be obtained by right from what depends on discretionary review or third-party action.

This matters because the construction start date can be a financing condition, not just a planning date. A lender may commit only after permits, contractor contracts and acceptable project costs are in place. If a sponsor intends to buy land before that point, the capital stack must fund the entitlement period separately and acknowledge the risk that approvals change scope or never materialize.

Build the complete development budget

Hard costs are only one part of the sources and uses. Include architectural and engineering fees, surveys, permits, impact fees, legal costs, utilities, insurance, taxes, project management, leasing or sales expense, lender fees, interest reserves, contingencies, and any payments required to satisfy prior debt or land obligations.

Illustrative Development Capital Plan

Why the total project cost matters more than the land price

  • Land and acquisition closing: $2.40 million
  • Hard construction costs: $9.50 million
  • Soft costs, permits and professional services: $1.30 million
  • Financing and interest carry: $850,000
  • Contingency and reserve: $950,000
  • Total hypothetical uses: $15.00 million

Suppose a preliminary construction financing model shows $9.00 million in potential senior proceeds and the sponsor has $3.50 million in documented equity. The remaining $2.50 million is an unfilled planning gap. That does not establish that a bank will lend $9.00 million or that the project's collateral, LTC, LTV, guarantees or takeout meet any particular policy. Those questions must be independently underwritten.

How loan-to-cost, loan-to-value and cash flow interact

Loan-to-cost (LTC) compares debt to the lender-accepted project cost basis. In the hypothetical project above, $9 million of senior debt on $15 million in accepted project costs is a 60% LTC ratio. If the lender excludes some costs from its eligible basis, the same loan produces a higher effective LTC. Do not assume every line in the sponsor's spreadsheet counts toward lender cost.

Loan-to-value (LTV) compares debt with the lender's accepted collateral value. During construction, the lender may examine current “as-is” value, an “as-complete” or stabilized appraisal, and other collateral conditions. The applicable test and allowed leverage depend on the product, collateral, regulatory standards and lender policy.

Cash flow is an entirely separate challenge. Development projects may not produce sufficient operating income during construction, and a completed project may require months of lease-up before permanent debt becomes realistic. Interest reserves, guarantees and completion support can help manage timing risk but do not create property-level NOI.

The Federal Reserve's Interagency Guidelines for Real Estate Lending Policies discuss supervisory loan-to-value limits and sound underwriting. Supervisory limits are not promises of maximum available proceeds; individual institutions and transaction structures may apply stricter tests.

Construction draws are part of liquidity planning

Many development borrowers underestimate the gap between having an approved construction budget and receiving each lender advance. Contractors may require deposits before the related work is eligible for reimbursement. Lien waivers, inspections, title updates and documentation can create time between incurred expense and funded draw.

Prepare a monthly cash-flow schedule that shows land funding, sponsor equity timing, lender advances, required interest payments and cash reserves. Identify which funding sources arrive first and who covers timing differences. If an investor has verbally expressed interest but has not committed, label that source as prospective, not available.

The contingency must reflect real construction uncertainty

A project budget can balance to the dollar and still be unreliable. Commodity prices, scope revisions, labor availability, underground conditions, utility delays and permitting changes can create additional costs. A contingency should be tied to the project's design completeness and risk profile; there is no universal percentage that makes every budget safe.

Test an upside and downside construction schedule. An additional six months may increase interest carry, property taxes, insurance, contractor general conditions and sponsor overhead. If the only way to cover a delay is an unspecified follow-on raise, the capital strategy has a visible weakness.

Construction finance should be sized to a credible takeout

Permanent refinance capacity and the expected sale market deserve analysis well before the first draw. For rental projects, examine stabilized NOI, likely lease-up evidence, DSCR, debt yield, interest rates and accepted collateral value. For for-sale projects, document presales or absorption assumptions, release provisions and sales cost assumptions as relevant to the project.

A development lender may insist on a completion guarantee, repayment recourse, minimum equity, an acceptable takeout or extension rights. The sponsor must read the actual conditions and evaluate how far contractual remedies reach beyond the property-owning entity.

For hypothetical income-based takeout sizing, the Leva commercial real estate loan calculator can compare DSCR, LTV and debt-yield constraints. It does not model construction draws, entitlement feasibility or project-level development LTC, so a separate construction budget and cash-flow schedule are still required. For an example of diagnosing a shortfall at takeout, see Commercial Real Estate Refinance Gaps.

Decide what each capital partner is being asked to fund

Senior construction debt, sponsor common equity, joint-venture development equity and preferred equity have different economics, permissions and controls. A land lender may not be the construction lender; a construction lender may not be the takeout lender. Some capital providers require a negotiated development management agreement, an experienced guarantor, presales, preleasing or a minimum sponsor co-investment.

Clarify who owns the land, who manages construction, who signs guarantees, how cost overruns are funded, which approvals require investor consent and what happens if an exit is delayed. Marketing the project as “fully funded except for the gap” is misleading when the gap itself has no committed solution.

The concise package that earns a credible first conversation

Provide a one-page opportunity summary, site control and land basis, entitlement matrix, project description, current plans, third-party feasibility work, complete sources and uses, monthly construction cash flow, procurement and contractor schedule, a quantified contingency, sponsor and team credentials, environmental and other material reports, debt assumptions distinguished from offers, and documented equity commitments.

A sophisticated capital conversation often starts with the unanswered questions rather than a proposed return. A financeable presentation identifies those uncertainties without dressing them up as completed milestones. This improves the quality of introductions even when a lender or investor ultimately declines.

Frequently Asked Questions

Development capital decisions

Can construction financing close before all permits are issued?

Some arrangements can be structured with conditions or separate predevelopment financing, but availability depends on lender and project requirements. Do not assume a construction lender will fund vertical work before conditions are satisfied.

Is senior construction debt a percentage of completed appraised value?

Not necessarily. Lenders may apply several constraints, including accepted project costs, collateral value, sponsor capacity, policy limits and takeout risks. The lowest acceptable sizing or other underwriting condition can govern.

Can the Leva calculator model a construction draw schedule?

No. The calculator models commercial loan payments, refinance constraints and illustrative funding gaps. Construction draws, eligible LTC basis, cost-to-complete, contractor and entitlement risks require separate development underwriting.
