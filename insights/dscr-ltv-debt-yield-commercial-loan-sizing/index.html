DSCR, LTV and debt yield: what actually limits a commercial real estate loan?

Three common underwriting tests may point to three different loan amounts. The smallest one can matter more than the property appraisal—and understanding which test controls helps sponsors prepare for a refinance.

Published November 10, 2026 · Educational analysis · Example figures are hypothetical

The mistake: starting with property value alone

When an owner hears a property is worth $8 million, it is tempting to begin with a loan-to-value percentage and assume the refinance will follow. A 65% LTV would imply $5.2 million of financing. Yet a lender may reach a lower amount because the property's net operating income cannot support the proposed debt service. Another lender may apply a separate minimum debt yield.

Good loan sizing is not about choosing the most favorable formula. It is about identifying all applicable constraints, confirming what income the lender will actually underwrite, and checking the practical consequences for the transaction.

Three limits, three questions

1. Debt service coverage ratio (DSCR)

DSCR asks whether underwritten cash flow covers required principal and interest. The usual calculation is annual NOI divided by annual debt service. At a required DSCR of 1.25x, $520,000 in NOI supports no more than $416,000 of annual modeled debt service. To convert that payment capacity into loan proceeds, use the actual assumed interest rate and amortization schedule. The result changes with the rate and amortization period.

2. Loan-to-value (LTV)

LTV compares the loan amount with accepted collateral value. At a 65% maximum LTV, an $8 million valuation implies a $5.2 million ceiling. This does not confirm that a lender will accept the owner's valuation, nor that LTV is the binding criterion.

3. Debt yield

Debt yield is underwritten annual NOI divided by the principal amount. If the lender requires at least 9% and the property generates $520,000 of annual NOI, the debt-yield loan ceiling is about $5.78 million. Debt yield is often useful because it does not directly depend on a particular coupon or amortization schedule, although its inputs still require underwriting judgment.

A worked example: an $8 million property

Assume a property value of $8,000,000, underwritten annual NOI of $520,000, a 7.50% loan interest rate, 25-year amortization, minimum 1.25x DSCR, 65% maximum LTV and 9% minimum debt yield. For simplicity, model fully amortizing monthly debt service with no interest-only period.

Modeled senior capacity: $4,691,080. This is the minimum of the three calculated ceilings—not a financing offer.

Now suppose the payoff on the existing loan is $5.6 million, with another $180,000 in transaction costs and reserves. Total modeled refinance uses are $5.78 million. Comparing those uses with modeled senior proceeds produces a shortfall of approximately $1,088,920.

This is why equity on paper can coexist with a cash requirement at maturity. The calculated market value is not the same as funds a lender is willing to advance.

What happens if NOI improves?

If the underwritten NOI increases by 10%, to $572,000, the DSCR ceiling rises to approximately $5.16 million under the same interest and amortization assumptions. That remains just under the $5.2 million LTV ceiling. In this scenario, the loan is still DSCR-constrained even after the income improvement.

Owners should distinguish already realized and documented NOI from future increases. A signed lease, projected rent increase or stabilization plan may not receive full credit until it satisfies lender-specific conditions, which can include occupancy, collections, tenant quality and lease commencement.

What a sponsor should do before discussing alternative capital

  • Reconcile NOI: distinguish trailing results, underwritten adjustments and optimistic pro forma assumptions. Identify vacancy, concessions, extraordinary costs and required reserves.
  • Confirm the payoff: obtain a current lender statement, including any prepayment charge or maturity-related obligations.
  • Rebuild sources and uses: include reasonable fees, escrows, costs and any reserve or sponsor-equity requirement.
  • Test rate and maturity risk: a loan that works at one coupon or amortization can fail under a different structure.
  • Evaluate capital alternatives carefully: sponsor equity, extension, a permitted supplemental debt layer or an equity partner may change the transaction, but availability, priority, lender consent and cost are independent questions.
